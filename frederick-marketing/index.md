<section class="case-study-act" id="framework" aria-label="Section 1">
  <div class="case-study-act-inner">
    <div class="case-study-act-number">01</div>
    <div class="case-study-act-content">
      <h2 class="case-study-act-title">One rulebook behind every automation</h2>
      <p class="case-study-act-intro">The finding that reframed the problem wasn't a feature request. Every owner already had their own unique follow-up sequence. Hardcoding each individual flow wouldn't scale, so I broke each one down into its most basic chunks, then mapped those against our existing flows to see if they still worked.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="major-section">
  <div class="major-section-inner major-section-inner--wide">
    <div class="subsection">
      <h3 class="case-study-sub-mode">Where it started</h3>
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="case-study-screenshot">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/frederick-automations/framework-whiteboard.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Whiteboard working session: a list of every automation, notes comparing the current and platform versions of Fill Slow Days and Boost, and a Frederick decision tree of skip conditions">
        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>Auditing what we had</strong> Every automation on the wall, then two questions asked of each: what does it decide, and what does the owner decide? The decision tree at the bottom is the path they all turned out to share, communication history, appointment data, business settings, user preference, with a skip at every gate. Drawing it settled the question. These weren't nine products. They were one product with different rules loaded in.</p>
      </div>

      <h3 class="case-study-sub-mode">The formula of Frederick</h3>
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/frederick-automations/framework-objects.png' | relative_url }}" alt="The automation framework: five blocks grouped into When/Why, Who, and What feeding Campaigns and Automation, beside a formula showing four combinations of those blocks">
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 3.05%; --y: 12.9%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 3.05%; --y: 28.1%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 3.05%; --y: 51.9%;">3</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 3.05%; --y: 75.5%;">4</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 3.05%; --y: 90.7%;">5</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 35.6%; --y: 52%;">6</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> <strong>Distribution Rulebook.</strong> Frederick's brain. Open it up and see exactly when and why something sends.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>User Triggered.</strong> Not really an object. I broke it out so manual control never got designed out.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> <strong>Contacts.</strong> Lists, segmentation, CRM. Under every single send, which is why smart lists mattered.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> <strong>Offer Rulebook.</strong> What actually gets sent. Fully customizable, so no discount goes out without approval.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">5</span> <strong>One Off Content.</strong> The escape hatch for when the user wants to own the whole process.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">6</span> <strong>Automation vs Campaign.</strong> Frederick decides when an automation sends. The user decides when a campaign does.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

      <h3 class="case-study-sub-mode">Mapping the formula to our flows</h3>
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure cs-callout-figure--half">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/frederick-automations/framework-map.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Every shipped feature mapped back to the blocks it uses: the five blocks grouped as When/Why, Who, and What on the left, wired to nine outputs on the right, colored pink for campaigns and teal for automations">
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 33%; --y: 9%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 48%; --y: 50%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 72%; --y: 17%;">3</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 72%; --y: 61%;">4</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> <strong>When/Why, Who, What.</strong> The same three groups as the formula. Every send is one block from each.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>9 distinct flows.</strong> 6 automated flows, run by Frederick to keep the business running. 3 manual flows to let users add their flair whenever they want.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> <strong>Campaigns.</strong> Manually triggered by the user for things like Monthly Newsletters or Holiday Specials.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> <strong>Automations.</strong> Configured by the user, dynamically run by Frederick based on underlying conditions being met.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>
    </div>

</section>

<section class="case-study-act" id="trust" aria-label="Section 2">
  <div class="case-study-act-inner">
    <div class="case-study-act-number">02</div>
    <div class="case-study-act-content">
      <h2 class="case-study-act-title">Show them exactly what goes out</h2>
      <p class="case-study-act-intro">5 of the 11 wouldn't commit to an automated membership upsell. It had nothing to do with price or features, and everything to do with their brand name. They didn't want an automation saying something on behalf of their brand that they weren't aware of. So I built the settings to be read back, not just filled in.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> <strong>Who it's sent to.</strong> The rule written as a sentence you can read out loud. The builder sits one tap behind it.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>What to send.</strong> The Offer Rulebook, surfaced. Same automation whether you discount or never do.</li>

          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>Real names, not a count.</strong> The fear was never how many. It was which of my customers.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> <strong>Everywhere a list gets picked.</strong> No path through the product commits you to an audience you haven't seen.</li>

        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> <strong>Template first.</strong> Start from something branded, not an empty editor. The one thing the room was united on.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>One message, two channels.</strong> Not two campaigns to keep in sync. For a team of one, that's the feature.</li>

          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> <strong>Room for the caveat.</strong> Every business has a rule no dropdown will hold. Nowhere to put it means they write the email themselves.</li>

<section class="case-study-act" id="targeting" aria-label="Section 3">
  <div class="case-study-act-inner">
    <div class="case-study-act-number">03</div>
    <div class="case-study-act-content">
      <h2 class="case-study-act-title">Reach exactly who you mean</h2>
      <p class="case-study-act-intro">Owners described spending real hours pulling segments out of the booking software and pasting them into a separate email tool. Clients who'd taken a class a certain number of times. Clients who come weekday mornings. Age bands that decide which class a kid is eligible for. Frederick already had all of that data. The job wasn't to build another email tool, it was to make the booking data askable in the same language the automations already used.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="major-section">
  <div class="major-section-inner major-section-inner--wide">

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">

          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 92.5%; --y: 22%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 81%; --y: 50.7%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 81%; --y: 57.6%;">3</span>

        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> <strong>A rule is a sentence.</strong> Variables bolded, English with the blanks filled in. Same pattern as the settings summary.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>AND is a box.</strong> Rules inside it are joined. Nobody has to know they're writing boolean logic.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> <strong>OR starts a new box.</strong> The nesting carries the precedence, so it never reads like programming.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> <strong>Preview List, again.</strong> You never leave without seeing who you just described.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

      <h3 class="case-study-sub-mode">Two properties, one language</h3>
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">

        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>Contact or interaction.</strong> Who someone <em>is</em> versus what they've <em>done</em>. The second kind is what a standalone email tool can never ask, because it doesn't have the booking data.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure">

        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>One grammar, either way.</strong> Property, operator, value. Three dropdowns and no free text, so a rule can't come out malformed.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">

          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 36%; --y: 94.6%;">3</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> <strong>Lists that keep evaluating.</strong> Lost Customers stays current instead of decaying the day it's exported. The end of export-and-paste.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> <strong>System lists, locked.</strong> Consent is data the business doesn't own, sitting right where they pick audiences.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> <strong>Same builder, standalone.</strong> Define a list anywhere, then use it in any automation, campaign, or exclusion.</li>
        </ol>
