The problem

The mentorship gap.

How do we prepare students for a labor market that won't stop moving?

High schoolers are expected to pick a life path before they're even allowed to rent a car. Meanwhile, career counselors are so outnumbered that most students only get a five-minute check-in once a year. The result? Kids don't feel prepared, so they default to "safe" or "generic" majors like Business or Marketing because they simply don't have a guiding voice to show them anything else.

Audience · user personas

Three audiences. Only one of them is the hero.

The user

Students

Wants a guiding voice, and has never had one available more than five minutes a year. Old enough to be asked the question, too young to have the context to answer it.

So: the student is the only one who can move their own score. Orchie nudges, and never decides for them.

The champion

Counselors

Carries 450 cases and is measured on outcomes they cannot personally reach. Every tool ever sold to them added work.

So: Orchard has to produce something a counselor can use without assigning, grading, or monitoring anything.

The buyer

District leaders

Signs the contract, and answers to a school board for both student outcomes and the safety of minors' data.

So: they have to be able to justify the spend. That constraint eventually produced the core object of the product.

Hypothesis 01

The content-first strategy.

Students just need a better library of high-quality content and some gamification to encourage exploring.

Counselors need a way to track progress, view trends and assign homework.

Iteration 01

We built the library.

  • For students. We reached out to everyone we knew and filmed our own high-quality career interviews, built a gamified exploration experience, and added quizzes to prove students were watching.
  • For counselors. A way to track progress and assign "career homework" to their students.

Findings 01 · the engagement failure

First testing findings.

Didn't work

The search bar bias

Students didn't explore because they didn't know what to ask. They defaulted to the same three careers they'd seen on TV, leaving the rest of our database untouched.

Quiz friction

We thought gamified quizzes would prove learning. Students saw them as "just more schoolwork" and disengaged immediately.

Dashboard fatigue

Counselors were already drowning in administrative tools. The last thing they wanted was another system to log into. They needed a way to make their rare one-on-one time more effective.

Showed promise

Attention as a metric

Even when students ignored the text and the quizzes, they would still engage with the career interviews.

Visual authenticity

They weren't looking for data, they were looking for a vibe. Seeing a real person in their real work environment humanised a career in a way a salary table never could.

The scroll instinct

Students fast-forwarded through long videos hunting for the interesting moments, signalling they wanted high-density information in short, punchy bursts.

Hypothesis 02

Stop building a dashboard. Build a discovery platform.

With the research in hand, it was time for a reset. To provide real value we needed to move beyond a traditional management-style dashboard and evolve into a true discovery platform.

The shift was driven by one observation: students felt stuck at the search bar. When they didn't know what to ask, they didn't ask anything. By prioritising an exploratory experience over a search-heavy one, we could create a path for students to discover opportunities they didn't even know existed.

Iteration 02

Accidental discovery, by design.

  • Long-form cut into 30-second teasers. Focused on the most interesting part of a job, the "day in the life" moments.
  • A mobile-first scrollable feed, not a search box. By mimicking the social patterns students already used, accidental discovery started happening. A student would scroll for a "Business" clip and stop on a "Product Designer" video because the thumbnail grabbed them.
  • Counselors: from managing to conversation. A dashboard was just another system to manage. So we helped students build a personalised career plan before their five-minute check-in, turning a forgettable touchpoint into a high-value conversation.

Findings 02 · the false positive

The feed worked. We tested it on the wrong people.

~20,000

Educators we launched to, with analytics instrumented

Not our primary demographic, but watching them interact unassisted gave us the kind of raw data you can't get in a lab.

2 weeks

Of session recordings, reviewed every morning as a team

Letting us see exactly where users thrived and where they stumbled. The short-form feed was working and people were exploring.

We felt like we had a handle on the discovery problem. We had validated a student experience on adults, and it would be another full iteration before that caught up with us.

The shock

Generative AI hit mainstream. We didn't add a chatbot.

The world flooded with AI apps, most of them dense academic text blocks using verbose "AI lingo" to say very little. A technology looking for a problem. For a moment it felt like the quiet, human-centric mentorship we were building would be drowned out by the noise. So instead of bolting on a chatbot because it was the trend, we asked a harder question: in a world where everyone has an LLM in their pocket, is our problem statement still valid?

The reality

The ratio hadn't moved

Counselor-to-student was exactly where it was when we started. No amount of consumer AI changed what a school could staff.

The complexity

The job market got harder to read

AI was actively making career paths more confusing, not less. The question a sixteen-year-old had to answer got worse.

The opportunity

A guided AI, not a generic one

Generic AI was verbose and cold. A guided AI, fed by our career data and a human-centric philosophy, could be the counselor-in-pocket we'd been trying to build all along.

Hypothesis 03

From a passive library to a proactive mentor.

Move beyond a simple discovery platform to a truly guided experience: a mentor that walks with students from their first spark of curiosity all the way to a concrete, trackable future.

By adding contextual mentorship, we could use AI to bridge the gap between exploring careers and understanding exactly how to achieve a goal. That turns every piece of content into a deliberate stepping stone, and a library of information into an active roadmap toward a real-world career.

Iteration 03

Pivot to AI discovery. Still leaning hard on the videos.

We designed Orchie as a digital counselor that moves past the limits of a search bar. Rather than regurgitating data, it uses conversational context to help students articulate their interests and move from a generic label like "Business" into a specific, meaningful trajectory. Not an information retrieval tool: a partner that asks the right questions.

Findings 03 · the commercial failure

Schools thought it was "neat." Nobody committed.

The product worked. Students engaged with it, counselors liked it, and it still would not sell. For the first time the blocker wasn't a design problem at all.

What they actually told us

"We need a measurable way to show student growth in order to get funding for a new tool."

A district can't buy what it can't justify to a school board. The thing standing between us and every contract wasn't a feature. It was the absence of a number.

The answer

We built the number. District leaders perked up.

  • A credit score for career readiness. Instead of measuring static test scores or grade compliance, the CRI tracks a student's real-world growth across eight core dimensions.
  • Start with a rubric, not a chart. Levelled criteria first, so an abstract idea like "professional agency" became an observable action before anything got visualised.
  • Measure growth, not compliance. The thing school already measures well is the thing we deliberately did not rebuild.

Validating the model

I built a simulator before I trusted the score.

  • The model got complex faster than the design did. Once every chat, plan, journal entry and game could move a pillar, nobody could reason about fairness by reading a spec.
  • Synthetic weeks, real tuning. Sliders set the constants, a week of student activity, and the assessment baselines. The output shows exactly how much each activity lifted each pillar.
  • A dashboard on an unfair model is worse than no dashboard. If a student can grind one pillar and call themselves ready, we've taught them the wrong lesson and sold a district a number that means nothing.

Iteration 04

We rebuilt the entire experience around measuring growth.

  • Eight pillars, scored 0 to 400. Every chat, plan, journal entry and game moves one of them, so progress is something a student can actually see.
  • A spider graph, not bars. The shape tells the story before any number does, for the student, the counselor and the parent at once.
  • "Growth Report," not "CRI." The acronym feels like a test result. A growth report feels like something you can move.

The engine

Every message takes the same path.

  • Screen for intent and safety first. Before any skill runs. Everything else in the system is downstream of that one gate.
  • Four skills, one memory. Conversation, explore, compare and plan all read the desk and the chat history before answering, so a student never repeats themselves.
  • A guardrail lane, not an error state. Off-topic questions, distress signals and requests for a verdict each get their own handled path, including the conversations Orchie shouldn't be having at all.

The surface

Make the abstract concrete.

  • Life Sim spends the salary, it doesn't just show it. A student lives a virtual month on that career's pay against their real rent and bills. A number is abstract; four weeks of decisions is concrete.
  • Every path at equal weight. Four-year, bootcamp and self-taught sit in identical cards. The layout itself says there is no default path.
  • Cite the source or say you don't know. Every stat traces to BLS, O*NET or our own data. One hallucinated salary and a sixteen-year-old never trusts the thing again.

The surface

A plan you can't freeze in front of, and reps that fit a locker break.

Plan: one question at a timeBuilding a career plan in one sitting paralyses a kid. As a conversation they never see the whole mountain at once, and it lands at three horizons: 90 days, one year, graduation.

Learn: quests under ten minutesAnything longer reads as assigned homework. Every quest is tagged with the pillar it lifts, so it is never a random activity and the student knows why they're playing.

Governance

Built to be trusted with student data.

For a product used by minors and bought by districts, trust isn't a feature. It's the foundation.

Governance

Guardrails

We follow FERPA, and soon COPPA: student data stays protected, and Orchie never offers medical or therapeutic counseling.
→
  • Real-time PII scrubbing, so student records never reach the LLM.
  • Distressed-language detection that flags and escalates to a human counselor.
Our AI policy requires Orchie to cite verifiable sources and stay auditable.
→
  • Grounded in our own data plus federally compliant sources like the BLS and O*NET.

Where it goes next

What I'm watching, and what would change my mind.

Still unresolved

Cold start

The Growth Report only means something once a student has done enough to move a pillar. The weakest session is the first one, and the first one decides whether there is a second.

Breadth against depth

Explore, Plan, Learn and Growth all compete for a student who might give us twenty minutes a week. The pilot is the first time I get to see where that time actually goes.

One profile, everywhere

A shared profile is what makes Orchie feel like it knows the student. It also means a wrong inference doesn't stay in one place, and I don't yet know how often that happens outside a demo.

What the pilot tests

Riskiest assumption 01

Students will use Orchie as a mentor, not as a search engine.

The search-bar bias from iteration one never fully went away. Measure how many sessions get past a single question, and whether @ mentions and / commands get used unprompted. If they never do, the input is the problem, not the content behind it.

Riskiest assumption 02

A Growth Report that moves changes what a student does next.

Compare plan milestone completion between students who open the Growth Report and students who never do. If the score gets looked at but never acted on, it's a scoreboard, and the pillars are the thing to fix.
