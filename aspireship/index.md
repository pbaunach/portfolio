<section class="major-section major-section--problem">
  <div class="major-section-inner">
    <div class="subsection">
      <p>Aspireship trained people with no tech background for entry-level SaaS sales roles, then placed them with hiring companies. A restaurant manager, a teacher, a retail lead. People whose resumes were working against them.</p>
      <p>That gave the product an unusual shape. Most learning platforms end at the certificate, and most job boards start at the resume. We had to own both ends, and the design problem was in the seam: everything a student did while learning had to double as evidence an employer would accept later. I have led design across all of it since 2020, from the first free lesson to the internal dashboard the placement team ran the business on.</p>
      <div class="cs-impact-stats">

      <h2 class="case-study-act-title">Learn the Job</h2>
      <p class="case-study-act-intro">The course had two jobs at once: teach SaaS sales to someone who has never sold software, and generate proof that they can do it. Getting people to finish was the hard part, and it turned out to be a design problem rather than a content problem.</p>

<section class="major-section">
  <div class="major-section-inner major-section-inner--wide">
    <div class="subsection">
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Course-01.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship course overview with the first section unlocked">
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 32%; --y: 48%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 50%; --y: 69%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 48%; --y: 89%;">3</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 88%; --y: 18%;">4</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> Section 1 is unlocked for everyone. Free signups were converting to paid at 3%, because we were asking people to buy a career change before they knew whether they would even like the work. Giving away the first module changed the question from "is this worth the money" to "do I want to keep going." Conversion went to 15%.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> The upgrade prompt sits at the end of the free section rather than on a splash screen at signup. By the time anyone reaches this button they have finished four lessons and two quizzes. The ask lands after the value, not before it.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> Locked lessons stay visible with their titles readable. Hiding them would have been tidier, but seeing "The Demo" and "Handling Objections" sitting behind the lock is what makes the upgrade concrete instead of abstract.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> Progress reads 3% right after the first lesson. Tiny numbers early are motivating, because the bar visibly moves with every lesson at exactly the point where someone is deciding whether to come back tomorrow.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

<section class="case-study-act" id="match" aria-label="Section 2">
  <div class="case-study-act-inner">
    <div class="case-study-act-number">02</div>
    <div class="case-study-act-content">
      <h2 class="case-study-act-title">Get Matched</h2>
      <p class="case-study-act-intro">This is where the product stopped being a course and became a marketplace, and where the business model actually lived: matching is what made customer acquisition cheap. Two sides with opposite fears: the candidate is afraid of being judged on the resume they are trying to leave behind, and the employer is afraid of hiring someone who has never done the job. The match had to move both past that at the same time.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="major-section">
  <div class="major-section-inner major-section-inner--wide">
    <div class="subsection">
      <h3 class="case-study-sub-mode">The Candidate</h3>
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Candidate-02.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship candidate matches with double opt-in status">
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 34%; --y: 60%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 85%; --y: 67%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 34%; --y: 38%;">3</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 25%; --y: 76%;">4</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> The candidate answers first. A match never reaches the employer until the candidate has said they are interested, so nobody gets quietly shopped around while they are still employed somewhere else.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> Three states on one track: waiting on you, waiting on them, mutual interest. Job seekers read silence as rejection, so the interface always says out loud which side the ball is on.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> Base and OTE sit on the card before the click. Compensation is the first thing a sales candidate screens on, and burying it behind "View More" only wasted both sides' time.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> Match history stays on the page instead of clearing out. Past matches are the proof that the pipeline is real, which matters most during the quiet weeks when there are no new ones.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="case-study-screenshot">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Candidate-01.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship candidate match profile setup">
        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>Match profile setup</strong> A completion checklist rather than a wall of form fields, plus an explicit promise that preferences like salary goal and company size are never shown to employers. Candidates would not answer these honestly until we said so on the screen itself.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="case-study-screenshot case-study-screenshot--portrait">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Candidate-03.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship candidate profile as seen by an employer">
        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>The profile an employer actually sees</strong> A recorded introduction, an unscripted roleplay clip, the Aspireship courses completed, and the resume last. Career changers lose on the resume, so we led with the closest thing to watching someone do the job. Disclosing the project guidelines was deliberate: employers only judge a raw clip fairly when they know it was unrehearsed.</p>
      </div>

      <h3 class="case-study-sub-mode">The Employer</h3>
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Employer-02.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship employer candidate profile criteria">
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 41%; --y: 24%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 44%; --y: 37%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 48%; --y: 62%;">3</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 30%; --y: 18%;">4</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> The question the entire business depended on. Every employer had to answer it explicitly before matching began, which turned "we're open to non-traditional candidates" from a nice sentiment in a sales call into a filter the matching could actually use.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> Asked separately from the first, because "no SaaS experience" and "never held this title" are different risks. Plenty of employers would accept one and not the other, and collapsing them into a single question cost us real matches.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> Employers choose attributes of a person who succeeds in the role instead of years of experience. Our entire roster was career changers, so a conventional requirements checklist would have filtered out every last one of them.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> Match criteria live as a tab on the job, not in a separate setup flow. The hiring manager is already here writing the post, and asking in the same place is the only reason this ever got filled in.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="case-study-screenshot">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Employer-01.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship employer match queue">
        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>The employer's match queue</strong> Staff Picks first, with the Graduate badge and Aspireship achievements on every card. For a candidate with no relevant work history, that badge is the entire credential, so it gets more visual weight than anything except the name.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="case-study-screenshot">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Employer-03.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship employer job list with approval statuses">
        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>Job list with a real approval workflow</strong> Draft, Pending Review, Changes Requested, Open, Closed. Enterprise teams needed a hiring manager to draft a role and an admin to approve it before it could go live, so status had to be a first-class column.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

      <h2 class="case-study-act-title">Run the Market</h2>
      <p class="case-study-act-intro">Marketplaces do not run themselves. Behind both customer-facing sides sat the tooling our own placement team lived in all day, and it is where the honest picture of the business showed up.</p>

<section class="major-section">
  <div class="major-section-inner major-section-inner--wide">
    <div class="subsection">
      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="cs-callout-figure">
          <img src="{{ '/assets/img/aspireship-screens/Internal-01.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Aspireship internal admin dashboard">
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 60%; --y: 12%;">1</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 48%; --y: 44%;">2</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 48%; --y: 56%;">3</span>
          <span class="cs-callout-marker" style="--x: 48%; --y: 89%;">4</span>
        </figure>
        <ol class="cs-callout-notes-side">
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">1</span> Three numbers the placement team works from every morning: assessments to review, candidates ready, jobs to fill. Everything else on the page is context for those three.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">2</span> 83% of candidates inactive for 30 days or more. This single row reframed the roadmap. Our bottleneck was never supply, it was that people go quiet, and the "Interested in new job matches?" toggle on the candidate profile came directly out of this number.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">3</span> Placement count sits immediately under the inactive count on purpose. Seen alone, 20 placements looks like a win. Seen against 4,223 candidates, it is the actual state of the business.</li>
          <li><span class="cs-callout-num">4</span> "Jobs filled not by us" gets its own line. Nobody enjoys a dashboard that counts losses, but a marketplace that only measures wins cannot tell the difference between a bad match and a slow one.</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
        <figure class="case-study-screenshot">

        </figure>
        <p class="cs-screen-note"><strong>Internal job record</strong> Every job carries an activity log (posted, match added, interested, hired) attributed to a person. Placements played out over weeks and across multiple staff, so the handoff history was the feature.</p>

      <div class="case-study-callout-row">
